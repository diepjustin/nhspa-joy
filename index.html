How I Got Here

My name is Justin Diep and I am in my third year on the staff of Omaha Bryan's The Orator Newspaper as Editor-in-Chief.

I was always a good writer. Throughout my time in school, teachers would praise my writing, but I wasn't sure what else I could do with it beyond writing essays for a grade.

Sitting in the library in 8th grade registering for classes, I picked Journalism 1-2 as an elective because it seemed interesting — little did I know then that taking that class would change the course of my high school career.

I started my involvement with the journalism publication as a volunteer photographer for The Crusader Yearbook. The yearbook staff needed cross-country photos, and as a student who excelled in the journalism class, teacher Ranae Duncan trusted me to take a camera out into the field to shoot photos that did end up in the yearbook.

Since that moment I fell in love with the program. Our assignments to write stories also cemented my desire to join one of the publications. I wasn't really sure at the time if I should join the yearbook or newspaper staff, but Duncan encouraged me to join the newspaper.

In early 2020 I wrote a column about city developments within Omaha and Duncan asked if I wanted it published in the newspaper. It felt great knowing that my work was worthy enough to be published.

Riding this high I asked if I could write about the potential impact COVID-19 would have if it reached Nebraska for an assignment. Unknowingly, The Orator staff at the time was planning on doing an in-depth coverage piece on COVID-19 and I was invited to contribute as a freshman. The staff welcomed me with open arms and at that point, I knew I wanted to be with the newspaper.

The school year abruptly ended during spring break, so the COVID coverage I'd done never got published — but it gave me the experience that cemented my path onto the newspaper staff.

In four short years, I went from a guest reporter to now the one in charge of the entire show.

Career Goals

I plan to continue my career as a journalist into my college years and beyond. While the exact medium at which I present my reporting to the world is unknown to me at this time, I will use my time in college to explore all forms of media to figure out which ones I enjoy doing. Despite only having experience mainly in print journalism, I am very open to trying new multimedia formats to engage viewers and to better tell the stories that I report on.

Editing, Leadership & Team Building

I was always considered a leader by my teachers, but taking on an editor position my first year on staff was a little daunting, especially since I was the only new staff member that year. As a Copy Editor during my sophomore year, I worked with the other Copy Editor, Sports Editor and Editor-in-Chief creating layouts and editing each other's spreads. This paid off going into my junior year, when I became Co-Editor-in-Chief with half the staff brand new — I taught these reporters by remembering how the previous editor-in-chief had taught me.

Coaching and Editing

As an editor I wanted my staff to improve, and I found the best way to do it was to work one-on-one with reporters — walking through graphic organizers to find sources and questions to ask. When stories are ready to be edited, the writer uploads their doc to a shared OneDrive folder where I read through and comment, first on content, then on grammar and AP style. I sit down with the writer to explain the comments, then give them time to make the edits themselves rather than doing it for them, so they build the skill of finding their own errors.

Copy Editing

Even with spell check and autocorrect, errors can slip through the cracks. Before we send spreads to the printer, all of the editors come together for a final proof night to catch what the first round of editing missed.

Conventions

I believe what brought our staff together most was traveling. It was one of the few times we were all together that wasn't a late work night spent stressing over a deadline. Being away from some of that stress, just being people together, brought us closer — and these conventions allowed for growth through workshops, too. I've been able to attend three JEA/NSPA National High School Conventions with my staff: exploring Independence Hall in Philadelphia, walking down Hollywood Boulevard in Los Angeles, and visiting the Gateway to the West in St. Louis.

The New Advisor

The hardest part about my time as a student journalist wasn't the late nights or hard-to-reach sources — it was learning that Ranae Duncan, who taught me everything I knew about journalism, was taking a position at a different school. I considered leaving too, but decided I had a responsibility to stay with the program and make sure it survived. Before the new advisor's first school year, I met with her and the yearbook Editor-in-Chief to get to know her and shape the program around a mix of her fresh ideas and my own experience on staff, then met with returning staffers to set expectations that things would be different and we'd have to be okay with change.

Broadcast Journalism

I was introduced to the broadcast format in early 2020, before the pandemic, in my Journalism 1-2 class. When I joined The Orator staff, our online presence was more of an afterthought — the website was still new and the program remained focused on a print newspaper published once a quarter. Between our third and fourth issues one year, I had a little more time and took it upon myself to produce a video about a new boba shop opening in Papillion. I gathered footage at the grand opening, conducted interviews, wrote the script, and edited it all into a package, then asked a staffer interested in sports broadcasting to do the voiceover — giving her a chance to try a form of broadcasting she might enjoy.

As Editor-in-Chief I am also the webmaster for BHSOrator.com, developed in 2018 prior to my time at Bryan. Before this school year, the site was more of an afterthought — a place to archive our print stories. In addition to overseeing the newspaper, I manage our journalism department's social media pages. We had an established Facebook and Twitter presence running for a few years, which I continued to run, posting when we published stories and when staffers won awards, as well as department announcements. We lacked a presence on Instagram — my advisor remembered an account created in 2015 that never posted — so I decided it was time to bring it back to reach as many people as possible. Law, Ethics & News Literacy

As a school that practices prior review, everything we publish is required to go through the English curriculum specialist. For the most part censorship wasn't a big issue for us, but naturally we ran into a few issues.

Crime

A shooting occurred over winter break involving students from Omaha Bryan and Omaha South. A reporter wanted to cover it by showing the experience of a student next to the victim, tying it to a recent increase in gun violence within Omaha. During prior review, we were informed litigation and an investigation were pending between the school and the victim's family. We felt the story was still important to tell because it directly affected the South Omaha community, so to get it published we removed all names and left a footnote explaining our decision to readers.

Editorial Disclaimer

While an editorial disclaimer was printed in our newspaper on the opinions page, nothing on our website specified the official description of our opinion stories. That caused an issue when one of my staffers wrote a review on Dahmer — Monster: The Jeffrey Dahmer Story. Some readers were upset, believing the school was advertising the already-controversial show, even though the review itself was critical of it. After meeting with my advisor and the writer, I decided a direct response wouldn't do much good, but I did add our editorial disclaimer to the About page of The Orator's website and a disclaimer at the end of each editorial, column and review published, to make clear whose opinion the story represents and to separate The Orator's content from Bryan High School.

Commitment to Diversity

Diversity is very important to our coverage in The Orator, especially since our student body is diverse. The racial demographics of Bryan High break down to:

  • Hispanic/Latino 68.10%
  • White 13.50%
  • Black or African American 12.6%
  • Two or More Races 3.30%
  • Asian or Pacific Islander 1.70%
  • American Indian or Alaska Native 0.90%

All three years I've been on staff, The Orator's makeup has been very diverse, and I've continued to ensure the newsroom was a safe place for staff members to share anything they feel. I was shown the importance of diversity when I attended the AAJA JCamp in the summer of 2022, where I heard from industry leaders about the lack of diversity in the industry — often being the only person of color in the newsroom, unable to fully express themselves because of it.

Localizing a National Issue

Gender identity within schools is a controversial topic. When the Nebraska Attorney General joined a lawsuit with 19 other states over anti-discrimination laws about gender identity, instead of writing a straight news story I took a more analytical approach and surveyed how public school districts around my community handled the issue — finding a wide spectrum, from no formal policy at all to Gretna's parental-consent requirement on one end and Omaha Public Schools' and Bellevue Public Schools' practice of using students' preferred names and pronouns on the other.

Marketing & Audience Engagement

The work we do as journalists doesn't matter if nobody reads it. It's important to make sure our student body knows when content has been produced — especially since The Orator has at times been an online-only publication. Our main focus for marketing is our own social media, as well as Bryan High's social media accounts. I reach out to the school's social media team to periodically reshare our content, and notify staff members when stories are posted so they can reshare on their own accounts. Facebook remains our platform with the most followers and engagement, often drawing comments from students, alumni, staff and parents.

Print

In previous years when we produced a print newspaper, distribution was up to the staff. Once notified the paper had arrived, editors and reporters would count it into stacks of 15–20, then pairs of staffers delivered a stack to each classroom, stocking extras onto stands in the journalism classroom, library and front office. The student body was receptive — after distribution, kids could be seen reading it in every classroom, and I overheard students and staff discussing our stories in the hallway.
